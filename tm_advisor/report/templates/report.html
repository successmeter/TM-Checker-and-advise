The law

An application can be rejected if the trade mark is not capable of distinguishing your goods or services from other traders'. Examiners ask whether the mark, as a whole, has an ordinary meaning for those goods or services that other traders would want to use honestly. Evidence that the mark already distinguishes your business through use can be taken into account.

3. Similar marks (section 44)

The law

An application can be rejected if the trade mark is substantially identical with, or deceptively similar to, an earlier mark (registered or pending) for similar goods, closely related services, or the same kind of goods or services. Examiners compare the marks as a whole, as a consumer with imperfect recollection would see and hear them. Some situations, such as honest concurrent use or the earlier owner's consent, can be taken into account.

What we found

We screened {{ r.marks_reviewed }} marks on the register in your classes and the classes IP Australia treats as related. {% if attention %}{{ attention | length }} {{ "was" if attention | length == 1 else "were" }} given a medium or high review priority, highest first. {% else %}None was given a medium or high review priority.{% endif %}

How we compared them: names by letter match (the share of letters that line up when one name is turned into the other with the fewest changes; two 10-letter names that differ in 3 letters are a 70% match), shared words, shared beginnings and sound. Goods and services by identical wording, broad terms, shared words, or classes commonly treated as related. These are screening signals, not the legal test. A review priority says how closely we suggest you look at a mark; it doesn't decide whether your mark can be registered or used, or whether either mark infringes the other.
